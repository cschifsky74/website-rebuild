When Should You Start a Backyard Project in Minnesota? The Month-by-Month Timeline

By Carson Schifsky, Owner, Schifsky Companies

The most common thing we hear in May is “we want it done by the Fourth of July,” and the most honest answer in May is “next year, if we start now.” Minnesota gives us roughly six months of buildable ground, and the companies worth hiring are booked into them well before the snow melts. The homeowners who get the yard they want, when they want it, are the ones who treat winter as the planning season instead of the waiting season.

Here is how the year actually works for a Twin Cities backyard project, and how long each kind of project takes once it starts.

Why winter is the planning season

Three things make a winter start pay off.

Contractors book spring over the winter. Design-build companies have a fixed number of crew weeks between late April and October. Those weeks are assigned as quotes are signed, mostly from January through March. A homeowner who signs in February chooses from the full calendar; one who signs in June takes what is left, which is usually August or later.

Design takes real time, and it should. A complete backyard design is several conversations, a site measure, a 3D model, revisions and material selections. Done in winter, none of that time comes out of the building season. Our design process runs the same way whether it starts in December or May; the difference is what month the shovel goes in.

Permits, engineering and orders have lead times. A retaining wall over about 4 feet needs an engineer’s stamp. A pool needs a city permit with a survey, a fence plan and, on lakeshore lots, hard-surface calculations (our pool rules guide covers that). A fiberglass shell or a louvered pergola is ordered weeks ahead. All of it can be done while the ground is frozen.

Our Lake Elmo design showroom is open year-round by appointment, and January is the easiest month to get an hour with a designer and walk on real pavers before you choose them.

How long each project takes

These are typical construction durations once a crew is on site. Design, quoting and the wait for a start date come before them, and weather can stretch any of them.

Outdoor living space: patio, kitchen, fire feature, pergola3 to 6 weeksGas and electrical trades are scheduled into the sequence

The order of work on site is not optional, and it is why the ranges are what they are: drainage and grading first, walls second, hardscape third, then structures and utilities, then lighting, irrigation, plantings and lawn last so nothing gets driven over.

Three real timelines

A patio and fire feature in Stillwater. Room to Gather, a patio and stoop renovation on an established lot, is the kind of project that designs over a few weeks and builds in two to three. Signed by February, it is finished before Memorial Day.

A backyard built in phases. One homeowner on our reviews page describes designing a complete pool backyard and building it over more than one season to fit the budget. That is a legitimate strategy: the full plan is drawn once, then the pool deck poured this year already allows for the pergola and kitchen next year.

A pool backyard swimming by summer. Poolside on the Hill in Woodbury is the model: the pool was set by our sister company Blaiser Pools, our crew was on site the day it went in, and the deck, walls, steps, plantings and lighting were finished the same season. The homeowner’s own review says they did not want to sit with dirt for a season, and they did not. That only works when the landscape is designed with the pool, not after it.

Planting dates that matter

Hardscape does not care about frost. Plants do.

  • Last spring frost in the Twin Cities is typically late April to mid-May; most landscapers wait for mid-May before planting tender material.
  • Spring window: mid-May through June, with irrigation or a watering plan, because new plantings need water through their first summer.
  • Fall window: late August through September, roughly six weeks before the ground freezes, so roots establish. Evergreens are the exception; we prefer them in spring or early fall and burlap them their first winter.
  • Sod goes in any time the ground is workable and can be watered, spring through fall.

Our one-year plant warranty applies when we install drip irrigation with the plantings, because water in the first season is what decides whether a plant makes it.

If you are reading this in spring

You have two good options. The first is a focused project this year, a patio, a wall or a fire feature, that fits a late-summer start, with the bigger plan designed for next spring. The second is to design the whole backyard now and build it in the fall, which is a fine season for hardscape and the best season for planting. What we try to steer people away from is a rushed design in May to chase a July date; the yard is going to be there for twenty years, and the design is the part worth the time.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to start a landscaping project in Minnesota? +
Start the design in late fall or winter and plan to build between late April and October. Contractors fill their spring schedules over the winter, so a homeowner who has a finished design and a signed quote by February gets first pick of start dates; one who starts calling in May is usually looking at a late-summer or fall build.
How long does a paver patio take to build? +
Most paver patios take one to three weeks on site once construction starts, depending on size, walls, steps and features. The design and quoting before that typically takes a few weeks, and the wait for a start date depends on the season.
How long does a pool and backyard project take in Minnesota? +
Plan on a season. A fiberglass pool is set in days, but the complete backyard around it, grading, walls, deck, fence, pergola, kitchen, lighting and plantings, usually runs six to twelve weeks of construction, with design, permits and engineering ahead of it. Starting the design in fall is how a pool backyard is swimming by July.
Can landscaping be done in the winter in Minnesota? +
Design, 3D renderings, material selection, engineering and permitting all happen in winter, and that is the most valuable time to do them. Construction waits for thawed, workable ground, usually late April. Some hardscape and wall work continues into November in a mild fall.
When should I plant in Minnesota? +
The two best planting windows are after the last frost in spring, roughly mid-May in the Twin Cities, and early fall, about six weeks before the ground freezes, so roots establish before winter. Sod can go in any time the ground is workable and can be watered.
Will my project be done by the Fourth of July? +
If the design is finished and the quote is signed by February, a spring start is realistic and most patios and outdoor living projects are done by early summer. A pool backyard that starts design in spring is more likely a late-summer or fall finish, which is why we encourage pool families to design the winter before.
