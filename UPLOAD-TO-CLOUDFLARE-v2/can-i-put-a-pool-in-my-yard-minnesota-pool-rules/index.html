Can I Put a Pool in My Yard? Minnesota Pool Permits, Fences, Setbacks and Other Rules Explained

By Carson Schifsky, Owner, Schifsky Companies

The first question every future pool owner types into Google is not “how much does a pool cost.” It is some version of “can I even put one here.” Twin Cities lots are smaller than they look once you subtract the setbacks, the easements, the septic field and the slope, and every city has its own rules.

This guide explains the six things that decide whether and where a pool can go on a Minnesota lot, with links to the city handouts we could verify. Rules change and every city is different, so treat this as the map, and confirm the details with your city’s building and planning departments before you commit to a design.

The short answer

Almost every suburban lot can take a pool. The real questions are where it can sit, how big the deck around it can be, what fence it needs, and what the permit drawings have to show. Those answers come from six places.

1. Permits

Every city we work in requires a building permit for an inground pool. Expect separate permits for:

  • The fence or barrier. Chanhassen will not issue an inground pool permit without a fence permit application attached to it.
  • Electrical. Pumps, heaters, lighting and the bonding grid around the pool are permitted and inspected separately.
  • Gas. A gas heater means a gas line and its own permit.

Small above-ground pools are often exempt. Chanhassen, for example, does not require a building permit for a pool that is 24 inches deep or less, holds 5,000 gallons or less and sits entirely above ground. Even exempt pools still have to meet setbacks.

Minnetonka adds a point worth knowing: anyone hired to do the work must be a licensed contractor. White Bear Lake notes that property owners may do building trades on property they own, with a waiver, but contractors performing more than one trade must be state licensed.

2. The fence (the rule that surprises people most)

A pool is a drowning hazard, so every city requires a barrier, and the specifics are strict. Published examples, as of October 2026:

A black aluminum fence disappears against plantings; a privacy fence changes the whole feel of the yard; and where the gates go decides how you get from the house to the water. 3. Setbacks and the things a pool has to stay away from

Setbacks are set by each city’s zoning code, usually by zoning district, and they apply to the pool, the deck or apron around it, and often the equipment. Published examples:

  • Shoreview: the pool must be 10 feet from property lines, and the site plan must show all impervious surfaces with dimensions.
  • Brooklyn Center: 5 feet minimum from the rear lot line and 10 feet from overhead wires.
  • Chanhassen: inground pools must be at least 20 feet from wells and 10 feet from septic tanks and treatment areas, and the survey must show easements, wetland boundaries and buffers.
  • Inver Grove Heights: pools are not allowed in front or side yards.
  • Several cities, including Eureka Township, prohibit pools under overhead utility lines or over underground utility lines.

On a large-lot property in Grant, Afton, North Oaks or Medina, the well and septic locations often decide the pool location before anything else does.

4. Hard surface and shoreland limits

Many cities cap the percentage of a lot that can be covered by hard surface, and the pool, the deck, the patio and the house all count. On lakeshore and shoreland lots the cap is tighter and setbacks from the water apply. Prior Lake publishes a 30 percent impervious maximum for shoreland properties, and Chanhassen requires a hard-surface coverage calculation on the survey.

This is where the backyard design and the permit meet. A pool with a generous deck, a patio and a walkway can push a lakeshore lot over its limit, and the fix is in the design: a smaller deck on one side, permeable pavers where the city credits them, or a lawn edge instead of hardscape.

5. Electrical, gas and the energy code

The Minnesota energy code, as applied by cities like Minnetonka, requires three things on heated pools and permanently installed inground spas:

  • A readily accessible on-off switch for the heater, mounted outside the heater.
  • Timers or controls that turn heaters and pumps on and off on a schedule.
  • A vapor-retardant cover on any heated pool or inground spa.

Pool wiring and the bonding grid around the pool are permitted and inspected by the city, and Ham Lake’s handout is typical in requiring an electrical permit for any new circuit serving pool equipment. Plan the equipment pad early: it needs power, often gas, a path for the plumbing and a spot that can be screened with a fence section or plantings.

6. The survey, the inspections and the fill

Most cities require a certified survey with the permit showing the pool, deck, fence, setbacks, grading and drainage arrows, easements, and wetland or shoreline lines. Chanhassen asks for all of it with the original surveyor’s signature. Inspections follow the permit card, and the rule that catches people in a hurry is the fill: Chanhassen will not allow the pool to be filled until the permanent fence or a compliant temporary fence is installed.

Before any digging, Prior Lake’s handout makes the point every city would: call Gopher State One Call at 811.

A pre-call checklist

Before you call a pool company or a landscaper, pull together:

  1. Your survey. The certificate of survey from when you bought the house shows lot lines, easements and often the well and septic. The city will want a current one for the permit.
  2. HOA rules. Many Woodbury, Hugo, Victoria and Chanhassen neighborhoods have architectural review on top of the city permit.
  3. Where the water goes. Which way the yard drains, where the downspouts discharge, whether the low spot is where the pool would go.
  4. Access. An excavator and a fiberglass shell on a crane need a path to the backyard. A 10-foot gap between the house and the lot line is comfortable; a 6-foot side yard changes the plan.
  5. The equipment spot. Power, gas, a drain and a place to hide it.
  6. The whole backyard. The pool is the centerpiece, but the deck, the fence, the shade, the kitchen and the plantings are most of the budget and all of the permit drawing. Our pool cost guide explains why we recommend budgeting for the space around the pool from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit for an inground pool in Minnesota? +
Yes. Every Twin Cities city we work in requires a building permit for an inground pool, and most require separate permits for the fence, the electrical work and a gas line to a heater. Some cities will not issue the pool permit until the fence permit is filed with it. Small above-ground pools (for example 24 inches deep or less) are often exempt, but still have to meet setbacks.
How tall does a pool fence have to be in Minnesota? +
It depends on the city. Published examples: Minnetonka and Chanhassen require a non-climbable fence at least 5 feet tall, Shorewood at least 4 feet, St. Michael 6 feet. Gates must be self-closing and self-latching almost everywhere, and openings are limited so a small sphere cannot pass through. Check your own city's handout before ordering a fence.
How far does a pool have to be from the property line? +
Setbacks are set by each city's zoning code and often by zoning district. Published examples run from 5 feet from a rear lot line (Brooklyn Center) to 10 feet from property lines (Shoreview). Many cities also set distances from overhead wires, easements, wells (Chanhassen: 20 feet) and septic systems (Chanhassen: 10 feet). Your city's planning department can tell you the numbers for your lot.
Can I build a pool on a lakeshore lot? +
Usually, but shoreland lots carry extra limits: a maximum percentage of the lot that can be hard surface (Prior Lake publishes a 30 percent impervious limit for shoreland properties), setbacks from the ordinary high water level, and wetland buffers. The pool, deck and patio all count toward hard surface, so the design has to be worked out before the permit.
Does a heated pool need a cover in Minnesota? +
Yes. The Minnesota energy code requires heated pools and permanently installed inground spas to have a vapor-retardant cover, and heaters must have an accessible on-off switch and timers on pumps and heaters. Cities apply this through the building permit.
Who should I talk to first, the pool company or the landscaper? +
Ideally both at once, which is how we work with our sister company Blaiser Pools. The pool sets the footprint, but the fence, deck, grading, equipment pad, retaining walls and plantings are what the permit drawings, the setbacks and the hard-surface math are really about. Designing them together avoids a pool that fits the lot but leaves no room for the backyard around it.
